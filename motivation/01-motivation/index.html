    Why I Built the Cluster: Motivation, Objectives, and the Alternatives I Weighed

    A foundational document. Written after the fact, but describing the reasoning as it stood when I decided to build.

    1. The motivation

    The honest starting point: I'm a CS student in Singapore interest in the domains of computer systems, networks and graphics. I am primarily gearing towards CDN, SRE, and platform-engineering roles, and those roles have a chicken-and-egg problem. They want people who've operated real infrastructure: GitOps, observability, distributed storage, ingress, networking. The problem is that you don't get to operate real infrastructure until someone hires you into a role that lets you. Coursework doesn't fill that gap, neither does a tidy GitHub repo full of toy projects that never ran anywhere.

    Over my 2 years as an undergrad in NUS (National University of Singapore), I have come to realise that the programming in CS isnt exactly the barrier for your projects. In the age of AI and thousands of projects executed through coursework or hackathons, you will come to realise what ultimately matters is that your project actually ships out and remain reliable during its lifecycle.

    So here comes the question of how. To do that, we must first somewhat understand the development lifecycle of software, from gathering the requirements to deployment and the monitoring + maintenance that comes after.

    As a student, you will typically learn the steps from Ideation all the way to development and perhaps the steps heading towards deployment. But have though about how you will actually serve your products to your "customers"?

    Suppose that you are making a website. Ask yourself these questions: - How is my website hosted? - How does the domain name actually send user traffic to the place that hosts the website? - How is the website backend server actually implemented on the physical device? Afterall, your server must exist as a physical computer - How do we actually reconcile bugs or even service failures during deployments? Or better yet, how do we anticipate and plan for such cases?

    There are many questions not yet asked for the average student like me trying to build our own projects that ultimately ends up in the dumpster due to the lack of deployment infrastructure. Answering these questions won't happen in a single weekend, it takes significant amount of time to grasp the IT infrastructure that exists within our world.

    So the cluster exists to manufacture the experience I can't otherwise get. It's a place to make production-grade mistakes on my own hardware, on my own time, where the blast radius is my weekend and not a company's SLA. Every hard thing I'd eventually be expected to know on the job, why a CRD sync fails, how to keep a database's backups honest, what happens when self-heal fights a manually-edited secret, I wanted to have already hit, understood, and fixed at least once.

    There's a second motivation underneath the career one, which is that I actually enjoy this. The cluster is a standing invitation to learn GoLang, to hand-write a sync protocol, to understand networking from the packet up. The portfolio framing keeps me honest about scope and sequencing, but the reason I keep coming back to it on weekends is that it's genuinely interesting to build (This is very important for us to not just suddenly burn out and drop the project altogether).

    Furthermore, it also provides a highly available infrastructure to actually experiment, and most importantly, deploy any projects that you make. This itself would perhaps give you a sort of understand and appreciation for the infrastructure that has been built from the ground-up to make our daily lives possible (and also to give us CS students a job). You will come to see how many people have contributed towards the effort, especially in the world of open source, the millions of unpaid contributor keep up the dependencies that we rely on

    2. The objectives

    Concretely, I wanted the cluster to demonstrate, and force me to learn the following, in roughly this order of priority:

    • Container orchestration in practice, not in theory. A real K3s cluster across heterogeneous nodes, with workloads that actually have to be scheduled, placed, and kept alive.
    • GitOps as the operating discipline. Git as the source of truth, ArgoCD reconciling the cluster to match, CI building and pushing images. The whole git push → build → deploy loop, with no manual kubectl in the critical path.
    • Observability that means something. Prometheus scraping real services, Grafana dashboards I actually read, an understanding of the RED method and how metrics feed autoscaling, not a default dashboard I imported and forgot.
    • Networking depth. Reverse proxies and ingress (Traefik), load balancing (MetalLB), secure external access without opening my router (Cloudflare Tunnel), and a high-throughput private path (WireGuard/Tailscale). Eventually hand-building an L4/L7 proxy and a custom binary protocol to understand what the off-the-shelf tools are doing.
    • Distributed storage. Longhorn for replicated block storage, ZFS for the NAS layer, and a clear rule about which owns what.
    • Real, self-hosted services running on all of it, a portfolio site, a media server (Jellyfin), a file-sync system (Seafile), and my own hand-written Obsidian sync (obsync).

    The meta-objective sitting above all of these: be able to explain why every choice was made. A cluster I can't reason about is just a pile of YAML. The value, to me and to an interviewer, is in the decision log, not the uptime number.

    3. The alternatives: different ways of acquiring compute

    Before committing to owned hardware I did genuinely consider the alternatives. "Build a homelab" was the answer, but it wasn't the only option, and the reasons the others lost are part of the justification.

    3.1 Public cloud (AWS / GCP / Azure)

    The obvious default. Spin up managed Kubernetes (EKS/GKE), pay by the hour, never touch hardware. You might have even learnt this back in school, or played with it yourselves.

    Why it's attractive: zero capital outlay, real production-grade networking and durability, the exact managed services I'd use at work, and instant scale.

    Why it lost, for this goal:

    • Cost shape is exactly wrong for a learning cluster. A homelab is a steady, always-on, high-utilization workload, the single worst fit for on-demand cloud pricing, which charges a premium precisely so you can scale to zero. I never scale to zero; I'd be paying the elasticity premium for elasticity I don't use. (The full numbers are here, but the headline is that the AWS-equivalent of my cluster runs roughly US$11,000+/year, and my entire hardware build cost less than one month of that.)
    • The managed layer hides the learning. EKS is the thing I want to understand the guts of. If AWS runs the control plane, provisions the storage CSI, and manages the ingress, I've learned to consume a platform, not operate one. The whole point is to be the person who runs it.
    • The bill punishes experimentation. Leaving a GPU node running over a weekend because I forgot is a rounding error on my electricity bill and a genuinely painful line item on a cloud invoice. Owned hardware makes mistakes cheap, which is what a learning environment needs.

    Cloud isn't wrong, it's the right tool for spiky, unpredictable, or scale-out-fast workloads, and I'd reach for it in a heartbeat for a startup's production traffic. It's just the wrong tool for sustained self-education on owned baseline load.

    3.2 A single powerful server / one big box

    Buy one capable machine, run everything on it (maybe with VMs to fake multi-node).

    Why it lost: the entire point is distributed systems. Scheduling across nodes, replica placement, node failure domains, "which node has the GPU," draining a node for a reboot, none of which is real on a single box. VMs can simulate it, but they can't teach me what happens when a physical node overheats or a laptop's battery-removed worker drops off the network. Heterogeneity and genuine node independence are features here, not accidents.

    3.3 Raspberry Pi / ARM SBC cluster

    The classic homelab starter: a stack of cheap Pis.

    Why it lost (for the main cluster):

    • Architecture friction. My whole pipeline builds linux/amd64 images to ghcr.io. Going ARM means multi-arch builds via docker buildx — a real, ongoing tax on every image, for no learning benefit relevant to my goals.
    • Underpowered for the real workloads. Postgres, Jellyfin transcoding, a monitoring stack, dev containers — these want CPU, RAM, and real I/O that a Pi struggles with. I'd spend my time fighting resource limits instead of learning platform patterns.

    It is also worth noting that almost every single piece of software that you use or integrate has a compability with x86. The same cannot be guaranteed for ARM. That is the reason why the first iteration of the Snapdragon Windows laptops flopped rather badly in the market.

    (Worth noting: I kept a Pi cluster on the table as a separate project for CS3210 parallel-computing coursework, OpenMP/MPI compiled from source, where the arch concern disappears entirely. Different tool, different job.)

    3.4 Free-tier / student-credit cloud

    Stretch AWS/GCP free tiers and student credits as far as they go.

    Why it lost: credits run out, and they run out fastest on exactly the always-on compute a homelab needs. The moment the credits lapse I'm back to the cloud cost problem, except now my "learning environment" has a countdown timer on it. It's a way to try cloud, not to live in an always-on cluster.

    3.5 VPS fleet (cheap providers)

    Rent several cheap VPSes and cluster them.

    Why it lost: it's a middle ground that gets the worst of both. Still a recurring bill (smaller, but forever), still someone else's virtualized hardware so I don't learn the physical/node layer, and networking between providers is its own headache. The one place a cheap VPS did earn a role was as a potential WireGuard relay, a single tiny box for NAT traversal, which is a narrow, well-scoped use, not a home for the cluster.

    3.6 Owned hardware — the choice

    What I actually did: three heterogeneous always-on x86 nodes (a Lenovo M910q control plane, an i7-6700K desktop with a GTX 1080 doubling as the NAS, and a battery-removed laptop worker with a GTX 1060).

    Why it won:

    • The cost shape fits. Capital cost paid once, then only electricity, and at Singapore tariffs that's a small monthly number for an always-on learning rig.
    • Hardware availbility. These kind of used and custom hardware is rather easy to obtain at a reasonable price in singapore.
    • I own every layer. From the physical node and the OS up through K3s, storage, networking, and the apps, nothing is hidden behind a managed abstraction. That's the entire pedagogical value.
    • Mistakes are cheap, so I experiment freely. Freedom is worth every single penny.
    • It's a genuinely learning experience. "I ran a multi-node K3s platform with GitOps, distributed storage, observability, and custom networking on my own hardware for months" is a concrete valuable project, especially if you can genuinely reason about it.

    4. The honest caveat

    Owning the hardware is the right call for this goal specifically, but it is not apples-to-apples with the cloud, and I want to be clear-eyed about that. AWS bundles redundancy, 25Gbit networking, managed durability, and the absence of me crawling behind a desk at 3am because a MoCA link is flapping or some sync error in ArgoCD preventing the deployment from being updated. I'm not paying the cloud premium because, for a learning cluster, I don't need any of what it buys, and needing to learn all of that myself is the feature. If this were production traffic with real users and an SLA, the calculus would flip. The cluster is the right answer to "how do I learn to operate infrastructure," not to "how should a business run its workloads." Those are different questions, and keeping them separate is itself part of what I set out to understand.
