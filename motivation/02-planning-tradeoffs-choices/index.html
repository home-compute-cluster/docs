    The Initial Planning: Tradeoffs and the Choices I Made

    Foundational document. Covers the decisions baked in at the planning stage, before and during the early build, and the reasoning for each.

    The first document covered why a homelab and not the cloud. This one covers the decisions inside that choice: the hardware, the software stack, the architectural rules, and the sequencing. Almost every one of these was a tradeoff, and the tradeoff is the interesting part.

    1. The hardware plan and how it evolved

    The original sketch vs. what I built

    The initial concept, captured in the original spec, was a fairly textbook homelab: one Lenovo Legion laptop as control plane + worker, plus three budget N100 mini-PCs as worker agents, on a S$1,000 budget over 3–6 months. Clean, symmetric, easy to reason about.

    What I actually converged on is messier and better: three heterogeneous always-on nodes using hardware I largely already had or could get cheaply.

    • deus: Lenovo M910q (i7-6700T class, ~16GB). Always-on control plane. Small, low-power, quiet — ideal for the one node that must never go down.
    • nebula: i7-6700K desktop, 24GB, GTX 1080. Doubles as the ZFS NAS and the Docker Compose host (This thing only cost me S$270 and 6 hours of cleaning and troubleshooting).
    • legion: a laptop with a GTX 1060, battery removed so it runs 24/7 as a worker.

    Why these names for the machine? Well no real reason, I just find the latin names more interesting and easier to remember.

    The tradeoffs in that shift

    • Symmetric N100 fleet vs. heterogeneous existing hardware. Symmetric nodes are easier to reason about and cheaper to buy new. But heterogeneous hardware I already owned was free, more powerful, and serves as a better learning experience. Real clusters are heterogeneous; learning to place a GPU workload on the one node that has a GPU, or to keep replicas off the node that overheats, is a feature of an uneven cluster. I traded tidiness for realism and zero marginal cost.
    • The Singapore pricing reality. My early assumption that N100 mini-PCs were sub-S\$200 didn't survive contact with Singapore retail, where new 16GB/512GB units land closer to S$400, and the genuinely cheap path is refurbished enterprise USFF boxes (HP EliteDesk, Lenovo ThinkCentre) off Carousell. This is part of why leaning on hardware I already had won: the "cheap mini-PC fleet" wasn't as cheap as the spec implied.
    • N100 mini-PCs stayed on the table as additive nodes if a discount ever put them under ~S$200, specifically because they're linux/amd64 and wouldn't break my single-arch image pipeline (unlike ARM Pis). Compatibility drove that constraint.

    2. The software stack — what I chose and what I passed on

    The stack was chosen to mirror what these tools look like in a real platform team, while staying light enough to run on modest hardware.

    Layer Choice The main alternative I passed on, and why
    Orchestration K3s Full kubeadm Kubernetes. Too heavy for this hardware, and K3s is real Kubernetes (same API) with the operational fat trimmed. Single binary, bundled Flannel/Traefik.
    GitOps ArgoCD Flux. both are fine; ArgoCD's UI makes the reconciliation loop visible, which matters when the goal is learning.
    Ingress Traefik nginx-ingress. Traefik ships with K3s and became my single routing brain. NGINX will be used as web servers instead
    Load balancer MetalLB Cloud LB. Doesn't exist on bare metal; MetalLB is the answer for LoadBalancer VIPs on a home cluster.
    Public access Cloudflare Tunnel Port-forwarding. My ISP router was locked at planning time; the tunnel dials outbound, sidestepping inbound ports entirely.
    Private/bulk access WireGuard + Tailscale Cloudflare Tunnel for everything. the tunnel hairpins through an edge PoP and is slow for bulk transfer.
    Storage (block) Longhorn Rook/Ceph. Far heavier operationally; Longhorn is right-sized for three nodes.
    Storage (bulk/NAS) ZFS Plain ext4/mdadm. ZFS gives snapshots, checksums, and mirror integrity the NAS role needs.
    Secrets Sealed Secrets Vault / External Secrets Operator, overkill until I need dynamic secrets, rotation, or audit logging.
    Observability kube-prometheus-stack Rolling my own Prometheus/Grafana. the Helm stack is the industry-standard bundle, they work together and I might as well just use as it is.
    Database CloudNativePG Bare Postgres in a Deployment, or Compose Postgres. CNPG gives an operator that owns failover, backups, and WAL archiving.

    Two stack tradeoffs worth expanding

    • Sealed Secrets over Vault. Vault is what "real" shops use, so there was a pull toward it for the portfolio optics. But it's a heavyweight stateful service to run and back up, and I don't need dynamic secrets or rotation yet. Sealed Secrets fits GitOps natively, encrypted secrets committed straight to Git, only the in-cluster controller can decrypt. The tradeoff I accepted: I must back up the controller's private key outside the cluster and outside Git, because losing it makes every sealed secret unrecoverable. That single backup is the load- bearing discipline of the whole choice. I will definitely switch over to Vault once a significant number of services warrant its use

    • CNPG over a shared Postgres. I decided on one CNPG Cluster per application rather than a single shared instance. Shared is cheaper on resources; per-app is cleaner blast-radius isolation and matches how you'd actually run databases in production. For a learning cluster, the isolation and the realism won over the resource saving.

    3. The architectural rules I locked in

    These are the principles that recur through every weekly log. They're the actual value of the planning, the rules that made later decisions fast.

    "NAS owns data, K3s owns services"

    The cleanest line I drew due to the nature of a heterogenous cluster. Durable bulk data lives on nebula's ZFS pool; services live in K3s. This is why Seafile runs on Docker Compose on nebula and not in the cluster, it's a deliberate, data-safety exception, kept out of K3s so it doesn't depend on cluster health. The tradeoff: a little inconsistency (not everything isin K3s) bought a lot of data safety and a clear mental model.

    "Cloudflare Tunnel = dumb pipe, Traefik = routing brain"

    All routing logic lives in Traefik IngressRoutes. Cloudflare and Tailscale are ingress pipes that feed Traefik; they carry no routing intelligence. This is what lets me have multiple ingress paths (public tunnel, private tailnet) without fragmenting routing across them. The corollary rule: a single routing brain per-app proxies (like nginx fronting an out-of-cluster stack) are allowed only if scoped to that stack, never promoted to a second general-purpose ingress.

    Longhorn cannot use ZFS (We should perhaps help with this implmentation)

    A hard technical constraint I learned the hard way: Longhorn needs a dedicated disk formatted ext4/XFS at a stable mount path with multipathd blacklisted. It cannot sit on a ZFS dataset. This shaped the whole storage-hardware plan, I couldn't just carve Longhorn space out of nebula's ZFS mirror (and ZFS can't shrink a vdev in place anyway, so clawing space back would mean breaking redundancy or a full backup-destroy-restore). The clean answer was buy/acquire dedicated disks, which is what I did.

    "2 replicas = meaningful redundancy"

    Longhorn below 2 replicas gives no node-independent redundancy. So the target is 2, and since deus overheats (no replicas there), the replica pair is nebula + legion. The tradeoff accepted: with exactly two targets I have zero placement slack, but two honest replicas beats three where one node is thermally compromised.

    The GitOps repo conventions

    • Two repos, always. App source (+ CI) separate from config manifests. Single-repo would create an infinite loop, because CI writes the new image tag back to the config repo, which would re-trigger CI. Splitting them breaks the loop. This isn't a style preference; it's structural.
    • Pattern B (upstream Helm charts): just an argocd/<svc>.yaml Application, no apps/<svc>/ folder.
    • apps/<svc>/ only when I author manifests myself; multi-source Applications when a chart needs my own values + custom manifests.

    The IaC layering boundary

    Ansible owns below-K3s node state; ArgoCD owns in-cluster workloads; the two must not overlap. I chose Ansible over Terraform because my manual work is machine configuration (k3s install flags, systemd units, ZFS, sudoers, WoL), not API-driven provisioning, which is Terraform's job and which I mostly don't have. And I chose to write playbooks retroactively (the next time I rebuild a node) rather than stopping a working build to Ansible-ify it. Bandwidth was the tradeoff: perfect reproducibility now vs. forward progress now, and progress won.

    4. Sequencing: the deliberate order of operations

    Planning wasn't just what to build but when, and the ordering was a series of explicit tradeoffs about managing simultaneous novelty.

    • CI/CD before everything else. A working deploy loop is the foundation every later service rides on, so it came first, even before the storage that some services needed.
    • Longhorn deferred until dedicated disks existed. Rather than fake it on local-path (which pins pods to nodes) or bodge it onto ZFS (impossible), I pinned the few stateful workloads to nodes and waited until I had real disks. The tradeoff: temporary node-pinning ugliness vs. doing storage correctly once.
    • Portfolio site before obsync. Both need Go. But obsync also needs Postgres and a hand-written sync protocol, three new hard things at once. The portfolio site is Go + a few JSON endpoints: one new hard thing. Doing the simpler one first means I learn the deployment shape and the language with low stakes before layering on distributed-systems correctness. This is the clearest example of my core sequencing rule: don't stack multiple new problems in one step.
    • Guides written alongside the build, not after. Turning each hard-won setup into a reusable guide (CI/CD, Docker, K3s, observability) meant the next service was a checklist, not a re-derivation.

    5. What I consciously left open

    Good planning also means naming what you don't decide. Carried forward deliberately:

    • Control-plane HA: deus is a known single point of failure. I decided not to chase HA prematurely: two etcd servers is worse than one (no quorum), real HA needs three, so a future mini-PC becomes a worker to shrink blast radius first.
    • KEDA / autoscaling: gated behind Traefik metrics existing first.
    • Renovate: planned for dependency PRs, not yet stood up.

    Leaving these explicitly open, with the reasoning attached, is itself a planning choice. It keeps the scope honest and stops me from forcing premature answers to questions the cluster isn't ready to settle.
