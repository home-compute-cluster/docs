    Cost, Value, and the Reality of Compute in Singapore

    Foundational document. The money argument, what the cluster cost to build, what it costs to run, what it's worth, and how that sits against the actual state of compute in Singapore in 2026.

    All figures are current as of July 2026 and, where they change quarterly (the electricity tariff especially), I've dated them so a future reader knows to re-check.

    1. Capital cost: what it cost to build

    The defining feature of my build is that most of the hardware I already owned or acquired cheaply, so my marginal capital outlay was low. But to make the value argument honest, it's worth pricing what this cluster would cost someone building it from scratch in Singapore today.

    What I actually spent

    I wouldn't say this is a cheap project to make. But honestly, I believe this is one of the cheaper hobbies that you can have, and you at least end up with actualy tangible compute power.

    Item Notes Approx. cost
    deus M910q Lenovo Thinkcentre Mini-PC Bought online, i7-6700T + 16GB Ram $280
    nebula Used custom gaming pc Bought from a seller in Carousell, came with i7-6700K, 24GB Ram and a GTX 1080 $270
    legion Lenovo Legion gaming laptop Used to belong to Red Hat, sold off for free in their company marketplace $0
    Longhorn NVMe drives (×3, 256GB) Free used, from company IT (pretty good quality) S$0
    legion's WD Red SA500 500GB SATA NAS-rated, for the Longhorn partition ~S$103
    ZFS NAS drives (nebula) 2× ~3.6TB in a mirror $400
    Networking (switch, cables, MoCA) Omada switch, Cat6, MoCA over coax ~S200
    Misc (thermal pads, USB sticks, etc.) ~S$30

    The total cost is about S\$1283, not too bad at this day and age with ballooning memory and storage prices, and the single most important storage component (the Longhorn drives) was free.

    What it would cost to build fresh

    If someone replicated this from nothing, at Singapore retail (July 2026):

    Item Qty Unit (S$) Subtotal (S$)
    N100 mini-PC (16GB / 500GB, new) 3 ~400 1200
    — or refurb USFF (EliteDesk / ThinkCentre, Carousell) 3 ~300 900
    Managed switch (TP-Link SG108E class) 1 ~40 40
    NAS-rated SSD / drives for storage — — ~600
    Cables, USB sticks, surge strip, misc — — ~80
    Total (new mini-PCs) ~S$1920
    Total (refurb path) ~S$1620

    So a from-scratch equivalent lands right around the original S$1,000 budget under it on the refurb path. That's the capital number to hold in your head: a complete, capable multi-node K3s platform for roughly one thousand Singapore dollars, once.

    2. Running cost: electricity is the only recurring line

    Once built, the cluster's only ongoing cost is power. And Singapore's electricity price matters a lot here, because it's currently at a record high.

    The tariff (dated, re-check quarterly)

    For 1 July – 30 September 2026, SP Group's regulated household tariff is 31.91 cents/kWh before GST, or 34.78 cents/kWh with 9% GST — the highest in Singapore's history, up 17% from the previous quarter, driven by higher natural gas costs. It's reviewed every quarter, and EMA has flagged Q4 could ease or rise further depending on global fuel prices. For this analysis I use the all-in S$0.348/kWh.

    The draw

    The recurring cost is the always-on draw. All three nodes now run 24/7:

    • deus (M910q): idles low, ~15W.
    • nebula (i7-6700K + GTX 1080): the heavy one; higher idle ~50W, more under load.
    • legion (laptop, battery removed): modest, ~16W.

    Realistic always-on total: ~80W idle, ~130W with moderate workload. Do note that the high idle is due to overhead power consumption. The overhead is due to motherboard and other components (GPU is your worst nightware here), not the CPU (especially the case with nebula, with only 5W CPU idle).

    The monthly bill

    • At idle (~80W): 80W × 24h × 30d = 57.6 kWh → ~S$20/month
    • With moderate load (~130W): ~94 kWh → ~S$33/month

    So realistically S$25–33/month, call it ~S$300–400/year. deus alone is almost free (~S$4/month); nebula with the 1080 is the main contributor.

    The one lever

    The GPU-heavy draw on nebula is the part of the bill that moves. When the GPUs aren't working, that wattage is waste. Historically I had cluster-shutdown.sh / cluster-powerup.sh + WoL to spin heavy nodes down, though now that legion and nebula are deliberately always-on for storage-replica and NAS reasons, I've traded some of that saving for availability. That's a conscious tradeoff: a few dollars a month for a cluster that's actually up when I want to build on it.

    3. Value: what the cluster is worth

    Here's where the numbers become lopsided in the cluster's favour.

    The cloud-equivalent bill

    Mapping my nodes to the nearest AWS instances (a GPU-worker-heavy cluster like mine maps to g4dn.xlarge-class GPU instances plus a t3.xlarge-class control node, with EBS gp3 for storage):

    • On-demand, this lands around US\$970/month ≈ US$11,600/year.
    • On 1-year reserved pricing it drops to roughly US$7,000/year.
    • And that's before the Singapore-region premium: ap-southeast-1 runs meaningfully higher than the us-east-1 reference (a g4dn.xlarge is ~US$0.526/hr in us-east-1; Singapore is higher still), so the real local figure is worse, not better.

    The comparison that matters

    • Capital: my whole build (or a ~S$1,000 from-scratch equivalent) costs less than a single month of the AWS-equivalent on-demand bill.
    • Running: ~S\$20–33/month of electricity vs. ~US$970/month of cloud compute. The homelab's entire annual running cost is roughly what the cloud equivalent bills in a week and a half.
    • Over a year: owned hardware total (build + power) sits around S\$1,300–1,400 all-in; the cloud equivalent is US$7,000–11,600+. Roughly an order of magnitude difference.

    But value isn't only the dollar delta

    The cloud bill buys things my cluster doesn't have, genuine redundancy, 25Gbit networking, managed durability, and no 3am debugging of a flapping MoCA link. I'm not paying that premium because, for a learning cluster, I don't need any of it. So the real value has two parts:

    1. The compute-per-dollar, which is overwhelmingly in the homelab's favour for a steady always-on workload.
    2. The learning and portfolio value, which is the actual point. The cluster is a standing, defensible demonstration of platform engineering — every post-mortem, every open item, every "here's why this CRD sync failed and how I fixed it" is evidence I can operate and reason about infrastructure. That's worth more to my career goal than the dollar saving, and it's something no amount of cloud credit would have produced, because the cloud hides the exact layers I needed to learn.

    Put bluntly: I spent ~S\$1,200 once plus pocket change monthly to build something that would cost ~US$11k/year to rent and that teaches me the thing I'm actually trying to learn. The economics are firmly on the side of building.

    Another thing that I must preface is that building the cluster my way would have you touching the hardware and configuring very fragile components such as the BIOS and manually tuning the custom setup. If you're rather new to PC hardware, it is perhaps not very ideal to buy random custom PCs online.

    4. The reality of compute in Singapore, 2026

    Zooming out from my cluster to the environment it sits in, a few things are true about acquiring compute in Singapore right now.

    Electricity is the structural constraint on any local compute

    Singapore generates ~95% of its electricity from imported natural gas, so the tariff tracks global gas prices with a ~one-quarter lag. In 2026 that's meant a climb to a record 34.78 cents/kWh (with GST) for Q3 — a 17% single-quarter jump. For a hobbyist homelab this is a rounding error (S$25–33/month). But it's the reason Singapore is an expensive place to run always-on compute at scale, and it's a real input into why large-scale compute here leans toward efficiency and toward the cloud's shared-infrastructure model. Land and power are both scarce and priced accordingly. The lesson scales inversely: small always-on rigs are cheap to run; large ones are penalised by exactly the same tariff.

    Fun fact: Despite being an island with no natural resources, the electricity prices here are still cheaper than the UK or Australia.

    The cloud is dominant here, and the pricing reflects premium demand

    Singapore is a major cloud hub — AWS (ap-southeast-1), GCP, and Azure all have strong regional presence, and ap-southeast-1 offers 800+ EC2 instance types. But regional pricing runs above the US reference regions. For businesses with spiky or unpredictable load, or that need managed durability and compliance, the cloud is the default and a sensible one. The Singapore premium is the price of low latency to a dense Southeast Asian market.

    The buy-vs-rent line: when local hardware wins

    The principle that governs the whole comparison: cloud wins for spiky or unpredictable load; owned hardware wins for steady, high, predictable utilisation, because the cloud's core value is elasticity, and if you never scale to zero you're paying an elasticity premium for nothing. The signals that tip an organisation toward owning or colocating hardware:

    • Flat, high baseline utilisation (nodes pinned at 60–80% around the clock) — the classic "we're paying a 3–5× markup on compute we could own" realisation.
    • Sustained spend into the seven-figures-a-year range, where it earns a dedicated finance/infra analysis.
    • GPU-heavy workloads, where rental premiums accelerate the breakeven.
    • Heavy data egress, where cloud pricing punishes moving data out.
    • Regulatory or latency requirements that favour owned/colocated kit.

    Real companies have made exactly this move at scale, Dropbox and 37signals/ Basecamp are the well-known cloud-repatriation cases. The honest counterweight is that owning hardware demands headcount and operational burden that erodes the savings, which is why the common landing spot is hybrid: own the steady baseline, burst into cloud for peaks.

    My cluster as a micro-version of that same logic

    My design is the hybrid principle in miniature. deus is the always-on baseline (cheap to keep up); heavy GPU draw is spun up only when needed; Cloudflare Tunnel (essentially free public ingress) handles the low-bandwidth public surface while owned WireGuard handles bulk transfer. I'm making the same steady-baseline-vs- elastic-burst tradeoffs a real infra team makes, just at a scale where the whole year's power bill is less than a fortnight of the cloud-equivalent invoice.

    5. The bottom line

    • Capital: ~S$1,200 once, more with further upgrades or additional nodes.
    • Running: ~S$25–33/month of electricity at the current record-high tariff the only recurring cost, and a small one.
    • Cloud-equivalent: ~US$7,000–11,600+/year, higher still at Singapore-region pricing.
    • Value: an order-of-magnitude compute-per-dollar advantage for this always-on workload, plus the learning and portfolio value that was the actual goal — which the cloud would have hidden rather than taught.

    Money well spent. The hardware is already amortised many times over against the cloud line item, the electricity is the rounding error, and the thing I walk away with, the ability to operate and reason about a real platform, is exactly what I set out to buy.

    You would perhaps argue the true resource being spent here is time. That is correct. But it is still time well spent learning the infrastructure in a practical manner, as well as a hobby worth exploring.

    Tariff figure: SP Group regulated tariff, Q3 2026 (1 Jul–30 Sep), 34.78 ¢/kWh incl. GST — re-check quarterly. Cloud figures: AWS on-demand, us-east-1 reference with note that ap-southeast-1 runs higher; re-verify at aws.amazon.com/ec2/pricing before quoting.
